Case study · AI ContentOps research

Where content workflows break down

I followed requests across five teams to find where missing context, unclear ownership, and late reviews created extra work.

Follow the request

I wanted to understand why content work often felt reactive. I used a structured workbook, interviews, and workflow mapping to identify 13 points of friction across five teams. I followed where requests arrived, what details came with them, when someone could make a decision, and how it got to engineering.

Where the process broke down

Scope was still moving when people asked for final copy. Intake and handoff varied by team. Meetings ate into time for focused work, and teams had no shared view of content quality or post-launch performance.

I treated earlier intake as the first opportunity. It gives a team a place to resolve scope, system behavior, and ownership before handoff. It also gives later reviews a record of why the team made a decision.

What I proposed

FindingProposed change
Intake depended on who askedIntroduce a shared brief for the user decision, scope, dependencies, and review owners.
Decisions disappeared into meetingsRecord the decision, rationale, and owner. Use asynchronous review when the question can be answered from the record.
Component content drifted across teamsReuse content patterns and connect them to design-system components and a shared style guide.
Post-launch feedback arrived unevenlyAgree on what teams will track, such as recurring support questions and content QA findings. Set a review cadence.

Where I’d test AI assistance

One proposal was a tool to update repeated copy across multiple Figma files. An edit in the wrong state or component can be costly, so I’d start with a narrow task. Find exact matches for an approved pattern, preview the affected screens, and ask a reviewer to check the changes before applying them. Keep the copy decision and the change history in the same record.

My pilot proposal compares this workflow with a manual update. I’d track time to update, missed instances, and incorrect changes flagged in QA. I would use those results to decide whether to widen the pilot, narrow the matching rules, or keep the manual process.

The work helped me see content quality as a property of the whole workflow. A shared brief can fix a problem that good line editing will never get a chance to touch.

Start with the handoff

Give each request an owner, enough context to start, and a review path. Test any proposed automation against that clearer workflow.
