Customer story · Independent adaptation

Why Ada split its retrieval workloads

Ada found different answers to two questions. How quickly can an agent retrieve information during a call, and how efficiently can the team index a knowledge base?

Concept topicUnderstand why Ada evaluated live retrieval and offline indexing separately.

Why a customer would care about retrieval

Ada builds AI customer service agents. A live call needs a quick answer, while preparing a large help library for search is background work. This adaptation of Cohere’s published customer story explains why Ada evaluated those jobs separately.

Start with the Cohere overview and journey map

On a support call, a pause can feel like the line dropped. For Ada, which builds AI customer service agents, part of that silence can come from retrieval. The agent needs to find the customer’s policy, procedure, or product information before it can use it to answer.

That gave Ada’s team a specific engineering problem. The system had to find relevant information and fit inside a voice workflow’s latency budget. In the background, the team also needed to turn large knowledge bases into searchable indexes. Each job made different demands on the same part of the system.

One evaluation, two deployment decisions

Cohere’s published case study describes Ada’s comparison of four approaches across 15,000 evaluation queries. The team evaluated online retrieval and offline indexing separately. It selected Cohere Embed hosted on dedicated Amazon SageMaker AI infrastructure for online retrieval. For offline bulk embedding, it chose Cohere Model Vault.

Cohere reports a median retrieval latency of 77 milliseconds and top-10 recall of 90.7% for the SageMaker AI path. The case study also reports that Model Vault delivered about 2.7 times the throughput per dollar of Ada’s self-hosted setup for offline embedding. These figures belong to different workloads. One describes the speed and relevance of retrieval during a request. The other describes the economics of building the index.

What the team learned by splitting the work

A single deployment decision would have forced Ada to weigh the cost of bulk indexing against the speed of a live interaction. Evaluating the workloads separately let the team choose according to the job each system had to do. For another team facing the same choice, the starting point is to measure how much time a user waits during a live interaction and how much work the indexing system does away from that interaction.

This is an editorial adaptation of a published case study. The results are as reported by Cohere for Ada’s setup.

Interview and approval notes

I’d ask Ada’s engineering lead for the scope and measurement window for the latency and recall figures, whether both offline setups processed the same batch and how infrastructure costs were counted, and one example of a decision the results changed. I’d request customer approval for the narrative, names and numbers, plus technical and legal review for performance claims before publication.

What to carry into another evaluation

Set separate quality, speed, and cost criteria for each workload. Keep reported results attached to the environment and comparison that produced them.
