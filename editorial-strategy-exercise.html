Steps at a glance

  1. Define the reader’s task and check where they get stuck.
  2. Use the evidence to commission a guide or raise a product issue.
  3. Compare completion and support friction before expanding distribution.

Independent exercise. All figures, tracking, and outcomes considered here are hypothetical. The method draws on my integration audits at Marqeta, audience research and intake work at Manulife, and evaluation documentation at IBM.

The decision

Imagine Cohere’s editorial team has published a retrieval launch and has one developer-writing slot before the next planning cycle. The options are a follow-up launch article, a hands-on comparison guide, or a customer story. I’d use the slot for a comparison guide and let the customer story progress through interview and approval in parallel.

What would change my mind?

Illustrative signalWhat I’d infer and check
84 of 400 eligible workspaces run a second retrieval configuration within seven days of their baseline.The paired-run completion rate is 21%. Check whether both events fire reliably, and whether access or integration setup explains the gap.
44 of 80 distinct evaluation-related support threads ask how to run a fair comparison.This is a specific question a guide could help answer. Inspect a sample of threads for product issues or access gaps that require an engineering fix.
Search impressions rise for launch queries, and internal site searches include questions about testing retrieval.Discovery and evaluation are different needs. Check search terms and destination pages before deciding whether to revise an existing page or create a new one.

If access is the main problem, I’d move the slot to an access and setup fix. If readers can start but get stuck comparing runs, I’d publish the guide and link it from the launch post and docs. I’d use the customer story for a separate audience: an engineering leader who wants to understand why a peer chose a particular deployment.

The brief I’d hand to the writer

Commissioning brief · Developer education

How to compare two retrieval configurations on the same corpus

Audience and job
A developer with a working baseline needs to decide whether to change how the application passes retrieved text downstream.
Deliverable
A short decision guide. Lead with when to run the comparison, name what to hold constant, and give readers a way to record answer evidence, latency, and input tokens.
Evidence and review
Use the versioned API docs and a reproducible example. DevRel runs the example and checks the fallback guidance. Product Marketing checks positioning and release status.
Where readers find it
Link from the launch and the relevant docs. Use the reader’s question in the title and answer it early. Keep API reference details in the canonical docs.
Next action
Run a baseline and a comparison on the same evaluation set, then record any trade-offs before deciding whether to adopt the change.

What I’d report after four weeks

Track the share of eligible workspaces that complete both runs within seven days of their baseline. An eligible workspace has run its first baseline and has access to both configurations. Count each workspace once and include cohorts whose seven-day window has closed. Report the numerator and denominator, coverage of event tracking, and the most common blocking questions. Search clicks and page engagement help explain reach. The paired-run rate tells me whether more teams are finishing the comparison.

I’d set an illustrative target of 30% against the 21% baseline. If rollout and volume allow, I’d compare teams offered the guide with an eligible holdout. Otherwise, I’d compare similar cohorts and report the difference as directional. I’d also track blocked evaluation requests per 100 eligible workspaces and review any increase of at least one per 100 before expanding distribution.

If completion improves and support friction holds steady, I’d carry the template into another developer workflow. If people open the guide and still stall at the same step, I’d work with DevRel to watch several teams attempt it and choose whether to fix the example, the placement, or the product flow.

Simulated leadership update · Week four

Keep the guide available. Fix the setup step before promoting it.

In this hypothetical trial, 120 of 400 workspaces offered the guide completed a comparison (30%), versus 88 of 400 in the holdout (22%). Blocked evaluation requests rose from 3 to 4 per 100 workspaces. A review of the example support threads points to a prerequisite people miss before the second run.

I’d ask DevRel to revise and test that step, then repeat the comparison before taking a larger launch slot. I’d report completion and support together and leave pipeline impact unclaimed.

The strategic reasoning behind the commissioning choice

A principal content strategist does not select a format just because it is popular. The decision combines audience urgency, likely value, evidence readiness, risk, and maintenance. I use the following questions as an explicit decision record.

  1. What decision is stalled? Separate readers who have not discovered the feature from those who started but cannot complete a fair evaluation.
  2. What is the smallest useful intervention? Check whether the fix is a missing prerequisite, clearer link, worked example, guide, or product improvement.
  3. What can we substantiate now? Name the release, sources, test environment, technical reviewer, and any claims that must remain conditional.
  4. What are we not doing? Defer a second promotional narrative if developer friction is the more consequential gap. Preserve space for a customer story once attribution is approved.
  5. What would reverse the decision? If evidence shows access errors rather than explanation gaps, prioritize onboarding or an engineering fix over the comparison guide.

Why this matters: It makes the editorial judgment transparent to design, product, engineering, and leadership. The exercise uses simulated figures to illustrate decision rules, not to assert findings about Cohere users.
