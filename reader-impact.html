Measuring reader impact

Test whether people can find the right page, explain what it means, and do the thing it’s meant to help them do.

Task topicSet up a reader-impact test and use the findings to decide what to revise.

Start with one reader, one decision, and an observable definition of success. The measures below are proposed examples for this portfolio and a product editorial team.

Choose a test for each topic type

Match the test to the reason someone opened the page. Give readers a realistic prompt and watch where they hesitate.

Run the test and decide what to change

  1. Record a baseline: Recruit readers who match the audience. Give them the same starting page, prompt, and success criteria. Score full, partial, and unsuccessful attempts. Report counts and denominators when the group is small.
  2. Trace where the problem starts: If readers pick the wrong link, check the label and placement. If they find the page and explain a claim incorrectly, revise the opening or example. If they understand the page and still cannot finish, check for a missing prerequisite or a product blocker.
  3. Revise and retest: Repeat the tasks with comparable readers. Compare success and time, and review any increase in consequential errors or support friction before broadening distribution.

Connect editorial performance to reader outcomes

Use behavioral signals to see whether a piece reaches people and where they go next. Pair those signals with tests or feedback that show whether the content helped.

  • Launch story: Track clicks on the next-step link and qualified registrations where available. Ask a few readers what the feature does and who can access it.
  • Customer story: Ask whether a prospective customer can identify the decision, evidence, and limits of the result. Track use by sales teams and qualified follow-up separately from attributed revenue.
  • Developer guide: Track completed evaluations among eligible workspaces, alongside blocked requests per 100 workspaces. A comparison group can help distinguish the guide’s effect from a product or access change.

How I review documentation quality

I use IBM’s public documentation quality guidance to ask whether a page is easy to use, understand, and find. DITA’s topic types give me a way to make the purpose explicit.

  • Easy to use: State the reader’s goal, verify facts and examples, and include the conditions needed to complete the task.
  • Easy to understand: Start with the main point. Use consistent language and move supporting context, methods, or review notes into clearly labeled sections.
  • Easy to find: Use descriptive titles, headings, and links. Make the information type visible and give the reader a clear next step.
